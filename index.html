Research

We work across aquatic, aerial and terrestrial robotics. Our aim is to make these systems useful to environmental scientists, combining sensing, navigation and manipulation with the infrastructure needed for field research.

Aquatic Systems

Surface vessels and underwater robots for water-quality measurement and biodiversity monitoring.

We study how robots sense their surroundings, manipulate objects and work together underwater.

Aerial Systems

Drones for environmental observation, precise landings and repeatable missions in the field.

Our work connects aerial sensing with a mobile hub and the facilities of the Flying Systems Lab.

Terrestrial Systems

Ground robots for visual navigation and manipulation beyond mapped environments.

We investigate traversability and planning under uncertainty so robots can operate on unfamiliar terrain.

Selected projects

Research in navigation and lake monitoring, alongside student projects in environmental robotics.

CueNav

Visual cues and video prediction for navigation in unfamiliar environments.

CueNav uses visual cues to guide video-based planning. Developed with MIT CSAIL, the method explores how robots can find their way in new environments without a detailed map prepared in advance.

Explore the project

ecoBay

Robotic monitoring of biodiversity in Bavarian lakes.

ecoBay combines robotics, water-quality measurements and environmental DNA and RNA sampling. Working with ecology partners at TUM and LMU, we are developing a system for repeated measurements at Ammersee and Großer Ostersee, with the aim of making long-term lake monitoring less dependent on manual sampling.

ecoBay in the bayklif2 programme

EnviroBotics

Learning environmental robotics through interdisciplinary field projects.

Student teams bring robotics and life sciences together to address practical environmental questions, including automated water monitoring. Supported by mentors and external partners, they develop and test their own solutions. The SVan provides a mobile lab for experiments away from campus.

Joining the lab

Our research connects robot learning, navigation, environmental monitoring and manipulation. Tell us which problem you would like to investigate or what you would like to build.

Theses, student projects and research

For a thesis or student project, describe your degree programme, the kind of project you are looking for and your preferred dates. Links to relevant coursework, software or hardware projects help us understand your experience. Student-assistant enquiries should also include your available hours per week.

What to include

  • Your CV and current transcript.
  • A short motivation and your preferred research topic.
  • Your intended start, duration and availability.

For research-role enquiries, you can also link to previous research or publications. Advertised opportunities are listed on the MIRMI jobs page.

Research collaboration

We welcome conversations with researchers in robotics and the environmental sciences. A useful starting point is a shared research question and a clear account of what each group can contribute.

Developing a joint project

Possible starting points include methods for navigation and planning, aquatic monitoring, underwater manipulation, or experiments that connect sensing with an environmental question. Describe the scientific problem first, then any proposed experiments, datasets or field sites.

A helpful first message

  • The research question and intended scientific contribution.
  • Your team, relevant expertise and related work.
  • Available data, facilities or field access.
  • An indicative timeline and funding context, if known.

You do not need a finished proposal to start the conversation. A concise outline is enough to discuss fit and possible next steps.

Discuss a research idea

Industry and field partnerships

Research partnerships connect environmental monitoring and conservation needs with robotics. Start with what you need to observe, measure or change, and the environment in which the system would operate.

From a field need to a research question

Tell us how the task is handled today and where existing methods fall short. Field conditions, available data and practical constraints are as useful as a proposed technical solution. We can discuss whether the problem calls for a field study, a prototype or a joint research project.

What to tell us

  • The monitoring or conservation objective.
  • The site, operating conditions and current workflow.
  • Your organisation and possible contributions, such as data, expertise or field access.
  • Your timeframe and the kind of partnership you have in mind.

An initial enquiry helps us identify the research question and the expertise a collaboration would need.
